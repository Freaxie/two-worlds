**Working title:** Why Didn't Ancient People Write About Dinosaurs?
**Runtime:** about 1:28 of voice-over plus a 1.5 s loop tail, about **1:30** in total
**Word count:** 231 spoken words
**Pace:** about 175 wpm on average. The investigation runs faster (about 190 wpm). The reveal and ending run slower (about 145–165 wpm).

> Timestamps come from a timing model of the final text (`captions.srt` uses the same model). After the real VO is recorded, conform the picture to the recording rather than the other way round. A take that runs long is covered by **06-editing-plan.md → Trim plan**.

## Delivery notes for the narrator

- **Register:** late-night documentary. Low-to-mid voice, close mic, warm and calm. It should sound like someone who finds this fascinating, not someone selling it. No "trailer voice" and no up-talk.
- **Notation:** `·` = short breath (about 0.3 s) · `··` = held beat (about 0.7 s) · `···` = full stop (1 s or more). **Bold** = the stressed word.
- Quoted phrases ("bones of giants", "dragon bones") get a slight drop in pitch, as if quoting an old book.
- The reveal line ("Finding a fossil…") is the thesis. Say it slower and plainer than anything else.
- The last line should **not** resolve. Let the pitch stay up slightly and cut on the breath.

## Clean read (for the narrator's copy)

> Dinosaur bones have been eroding out of the ground for our entire existence. So why didn't the ancient world write about them?
>
> Egypt recorded its kings. Greece, its gods. China, its comets. Yet no ancient text clearly describes a dinosaur.
>
> It's not that nobody found giant bones. At Mycenae, over three thousand years ago, someone kept a fossil rhino's ankle bone. Egyptians wrapped fossil bones in linen, perhaps as relics of the god Set. Emperor Augustus collected so-called "bones of giants." In China, fossil "dragon bones" became medicine.
>
> But most were mammals, like mammoths and rhinos: far younger, and far closer to the surface. Fossilization is rare. After sixty-six million years, most dinosaur bones lie deep in rock, and what surfaces is usually broken.
>
> The real answer? Finding a fossil and understanding it are two completely different things. Recognizing a dinosaur takes ideas science only accepted around eighteen hundred: extinction, and an unimaginably old Earth. Without them, a giant bone becomes a giant. Or a dragon. Maybe even a myth.
>
> Even in sixteen seventy-seven, an Oxford scholar examined a broken dinosaur thigh bone, and decided it came from a giant human. The word "dinosaur" wasn't coined until eighteen forty-two.
>
> So maybe ancient people did find dinosaurs. They just couldn't know what they were holding. How many discoveries came before we could understand them? And which ones are we holding right now?

## Wording choices that keep the script accurate (don't "punch these up")

| Line | Why it's worded this way |
|---|---|
| "no ancient text **clearly** describes a dinosaur" | A 4th-century Chinese gazetteer mentions "dragon bones" in a region with Jurassic rocks. Some palaeontologists suggest these may have been dinosaur bones, but the text doesn't let us confirm it. "Clearly" keeps the line true. |
| "a fossil **rhino's** ankle bone" | The Mycenae bone is identified as *Stephanorhinus*, an extinct two-horned rhino, not a dinosaur (Meier et al. 2024). |
| "**perhaps** as relics of the god Set" | The bundles at Qau el-Kebir are real. Their link to Set is an interpretation based on the site's cult, not something stated in an inscription. |
| "**so-called** 'bones of giants'" | Suetonius reports that the bones *were called* bones of giants. Their actual species is unknown. |
| "**most** were mammals" | Not *all*. Some Chinese "dragon bones" and some Native American finds probably did include dinosaur material. |
| "around **1800**" | Cuvier's elephant/mammoth papers (read 1796) settled extinction; Hutton's deep-time work dates from 1788–1795. |
| "**Oxford scholar**", not "professor" | In 1677 Robert Plot was not yet a professor. He became Oxford's chemistry professor and the Ashmolean's first keeper in 1683. |
| "**broken** dinosaur thigh bone" | Plot's specimen was the lower end of a femur. It is now lost, and is identified from his engraving as probably *Megalosaurus*. |
| "**maybe** even a myth" | Links between fossils and myths (griffin, Cyclops, Gigantomachy) range from plausible to rejected. None is proven. |
